# Relational Builder — where we are

*July 2026. Written for community builders, organizers, and neighbors — no software background assumed.*

## What it is

Relational Builder is a free tool where you describe something your neighborhood needs — in your own words, like you'd describe it to a friend — and working software takes shape in front of you, live, while you watch. A tool-lending list for your block. An RSVP page for the beach cleanup. A story collector for the elders on your street. You talk; it builds; you point at what you want changed; it changes.

You don't need to know what code is. You don't need accounts with technology companies, a credit card, or anyone's permission beyond an invitation. Signing in with your email includes free building, free shared storage for your app's information, and free hosting — a real web address you can text to a neighbor within the hour.

## What using it feels like

You arrive to one question: **"What are we building?"** You can answer it cold, tap an idea to start from, or plan first — the tool will sketch the idea with you, asking one good question at a time, before anything gets made.

The Builder knows where you are and who you are, because you told it once: your neighborhood, what you dream of building, how comfortable you are with tech. It sizes suggestions for an actual block, not an imagined mass audience, and it speaks more gently or more directly depending on what you asked for. It also carries the accumulated experience of the Relational Technology Project's commons — real tools, stories, and patterns from other neighborhoods — so your event calendar quietly benefits from every event calendar the network has already lived with.

Every app comes out looking cared-for, and no two come out alike: the look grows from your place and your taste, not a template. Behind the scenes, a second AI quietly double-checks each build against what you asked for and fixes real problems on its own — you mostly just see things work.

When it's ready, one click puts it on the internet. Your home screen then shows the part that matters: how many neighbors visited this week, and the notes they left you right on the site — a return channel, so you're building *with* people, not just *for* them.

## The newest idea: prompts as seeds

Software spreads poorly between neighborhoods — what fit one place rarely fits another. But the *description* of a tool travels beautifully. So Relational Builder treats the **prompt** — the plain-language description that would grow your app — as a first-class thing, the way gardeners treat seeds.

One click distills any finished project into its prompt: a few paragraphs that carry the idea, the decisions, and the feel, with the place-specific parts marked for adaptation. You can keep it, edit it, and watch its history as it evolves. You can share it as a link — and anyone who opens that link gets a fresh start with your seed already planted, ready to grow *their* version with their aesthetics and next year's better models. Shared prompts appear in the network library and on your builder profile, credited by name, and every prompt-grown app remembers what it grew from. The code you export carries its own seed inside it.

This is the remixing story the network has been building toward: not copying software, but passing on the idea of a tool so it can re-root and become native somewhere new.

## Connecting is part of the tool

A directory of builders who've opted in lives right on the home screen — with their neighborhoods, what they're up for, the prompts they've shared, and consent-first ways to reach them (book a call, or request an introduction that only happens if both people say yes). When your conversation overlaps with what another builder is doing, the tool may quietly mention them — once, dismissibly. And the RTP stewards are a click away for advising, walkthroughs, or building your first version with you.

## What this suggests for the field

- **The cost of a neighborhood trying an idea is approaching zero.** An idea discussed at a block party can be a working, hosted tool before the next one — which changes what's worth trying.
- **Software can be a commons, not a product.** Everything here can be taken out — the code, the data, the prompt — and nothing locks a neighborhood in, including to us.
- **The 90/10 holds.** The tool makes the 10% (technology) radically more accessible precisely so the 90% (presence, relationships, invitations) stays the work. The feedback notes, the visit counts, the directory — all of it points back at people.
- **Ideas can travel with credit and without flattening.** The prompt-as-seed model spreads *adaptations*, not clones — each garden grows its own.
